Guitar First Steps at Home With an AI Tutor

How to start guitar at home with no teacher nearby: tuning, two chords, the chord change, and how an AI tutor coaches a skill it cannot hear.

2026-11-02

Plenty of people own a guitar they do not play. The usual story is a few weeks of fingertip pain, a chord change that never gets faster, and a slow drift back to the case. A good teacher solves this by giving you one thing to fix at a time. An AI tutor can do the same, with one honest limitation: it cannot hear you. Here is how to make that work at home.

The first hour: hold it, tune it, name the parts

Week one is not about chords. It is about holding the instrument so your wrist is not bent into a corner, tuning it so your ear starts learning what in tune sounds like, and knowing the names of the strings and frets so you can describe what you are doing. That last part matters more with an AI tutor than with a human one. A teacher can see that your thumb is wrapped over the neck. An AI only knows if you say "my thumb is over the top of the neck." Vocabulary is how it sees.

Use a tuner app or a clip-on tuner. Describe to the tutor which strings were far off and which way. It will ask what you heard as you turned the peg. That is ear training, and it starts on day one.

Weeks two and three: two chords and the change between them

E minor and A minor are the first chords in the guide because they are the two easiest shapes that still sound like music together. The order of work is: get each chord to ring cleanly, then get from one to the other without a pause.

The clean chord is where the AI tutor earns its keep. Instead of telling you "press harder," it asks: which string is muted, is it buzzing or dead, where is your finger relative to the fret wire. Each answer narrows the cause. A dead high string with an A minor shape is usually the underside of the first finger touching it. A buzz is usually a finger too far from the fret. You find this out by answering questions, and because you found it, you remember it.

The chord change is a counting exercise. Set a slow metronome or just count aloud, strum E minor on one, A minor on three, and report how long the gap was. The tutor will keep asking for the number until the gap closes. That is the whole method: measure, report, adjust.

Weeks four to nine: strumming, a full song, more chords, reading

Once two chords change cleanly, the guide moves through strumming and rhythm, a first full piece start to finish, more chords and the capo, reading chord charts and tab, playing along with a recording, and finally barre chords and power chords, which is where most self-taught players stall. Each week is one guided hour, and each week opens with a prompt that re-briefs the tutor and tells it where you got to.

Keep a tiny log between sessions: what you practiced, what buzzed, how long the slow change took. Paste it in with the next week's prompt. The tutor builds the hour around it.

Weeks ten to twelve: pick a song, polish it, play it for someone

The last three weeks are a project. You pick a song, the tutor helps you break it into sections, and you work it until it is yours. Week twelve is demo day: play it for one person. Family counts. Nerves count as part of the practice.

Getting the tutor to behave

Every weekly prompt starts with a rules block. For guitar the important lines are the ones that keep the AI from flooding you with information. It has to keep each reply short, end with one question, and wait. When it forgets and writes a page of chord theory, type "Stop. One thing at a time. Ask me what I tried." It will.

Before you start, run the free placement check for guitar. It takes about 15 minutes of describing what you can and cannot do, scores you out of 100, and names the week to start at. Someone who already changes between four chords should not spend three weeks on E minor.

Questions parents ask

The AI cannot hear me play. How can it teach guitar?

It coaches from what you tell it. You describe what happened, such as the third string buzzing or the change to A minor taking two seconds, and it asks a question that points at the likely cause. It works like a teacher reading your description over the phone, and it works better the more honestly you describe things.

Is this for kids or adults?

The guitar guide is written for adult beginners, and a teen who is motivated can run it with a parent present. For younger children, the music guide for ages 8 to 12 is a better first step, with a parent running the sessions.

What do I need to buy?

A guitar that stays in tune and a tuner app or clip-on tuner. Nothing else for the first twelve weeks. The guide spends week one on holding and tuning the instrument you already have.

How much practice between sessions?

Short and frequent beats long and rare. Ten to fifteen minutes most days is the pattern the guide asks for, and the weekly hour with the tutor is where you review and plan.
