Homeschool Critical Thinking Curriculum by Skill

Choosing a homeschool critical thinking curriculum: reasoning, evidence, media and sources, through four guides for ages 8 to 17. Free placement checks.

2026-11-26

Most homeschool critical thinking curriculum is a workbook of logic puzzles. Puzzles are fun and they build some reasoning, but children often solve them and still believe the first headline they see. The part that lasts is a set of habits used on real material: what is the claim, what is the reason, who made this and why, and what would change my mind.

This page is for parents of children about 8 to 17 who want critical thinking taught on purpose. It covers what to look for and how our four guides divide the skill.

Who this is for

  • Parents whose child argues louder instead of giving reasons.
  • Families worried about ads, clickbait and AI-made images.
  • Parents who want reasoning taught inside real subjects.

What to look for in a homeschool critical thinking curriculum

Claims and reasons. A child should be able to say what someone is claiming and what reason they give.

Fact, opinion and evidence. Telling the three apart is the core skill.

The other side. Stating the strongest version of a view you disagree with is harder than winning, and more useful.

Real material. Ads, headlines, passages and historical sources are better practice than invented puzzles.

Changing your mind. A good course treats updating a view as a strength.

Regular practice. One skill a week, used again in the weeks that follow, builds a habit. A single unit on logic in one month rarely does.

Where our guides fit

All four are 12-week plans. Each is a PDF you run with any AI chat assistant, and the free versions of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot all work. Each week is one scripted session of about 60 minutes.

Think It Through, for ages 11 to 14, covers claims and reasons, fact and opinion, building a case, steel-manning the other side, spotting sloppy reasoning, sharp questions, staying calm, and changing your mind, then a family debate.

Don't Get Fooled, for ages 10 to 14, covers who made a message and why, ads in disguise, clickbait, checking claims, real and fake images, opinions dressed as facts, algorithms, and being kind and safe online.

Read to Understand, for ages 8 to 12, builds inference and the habit of backing an answer with the text.

History Is an Argument, for ages 11 to 17, teaches reading sources, causes, propaganda and whose story is told.

The AI tutor asks for your child's reasoning before it offers its own.

What they do not do

  • They do not include logic puzzle books or formal logic notation.
  • They do not take a side on political or religious questions.
  • They do not prepare a teen for competitive debate leagues and their formats.

Questions parents ask

What is a critical thinking curriculum?

It teaches a child to ask for reasons and evidence, to notice a weak argument, and to change their mind when the evidence is better. Puzzle books build some of that. Most of it is learned by applying those habits to real arguments, real ads and real texts.

Is there a homeschool debate curriculum?

Think It Through, for ages 11 to 14, is our debate and reasoning plan. The AI tutor plays a friendly opponent, and the last weeks are a real family debate your child prepares and argues.

Can critical thinking be taught on its own?

It can be practiced on its own, but it sticks most inside a subject. That is why our four guides each teach it through one area: arguments, media, reading and history.

What age should critical thinking start?

Children can start asking what makes them think so as soon as they can explain a choice. Our reading guide starts at about age 8, the media and debate guides at 10 and 11, and the history guide runs to 17.
