Homeschool History Curriculum for High School

Choosing a homeschool history curriculum for high school: sources, argument and writing, how AP U.S. and AP World History fit, and a free placement check.

2026-11-26

Most homeschool history curriculum for high school falls into two groups: survey courses that walk through world or American history in order, and source-based courses that teach a student to work like a historian. Both have a place. The difference that matters is whether your teen leaves able to read a source, ask why, and make an argument with evidence, because that is what college courses and AP exams ask for.

This page is for parents of teens about 14 to 17 planning high school history. It covers what to look for, how a skills course and an AP course fit together, and where our guides fit.

Who this is for

  • Parents of a teen who thinks history is memorizing dates.
  • Families planning a high school history credit, with or without an AP exam.
  • Parents who want sources and argument, not only a textbook.

What to look for in a high school history curriculum

Primary sources. Letters, speeches, photos and records are the raw material of history. A teen should read them, not only read about them.

Questions of why. What caused it, who gained, and what changed are better questions than when.

Argument and writing. A history credit should include writing a claim and defending it with evidence.

More than one point of view. Whose story gets told, and who is missing, is part of the subject.

A fit with your plan. Decide early whether this year ends in an AP exam. That changes the pace in spring.

Questions parents ask

What should a high school history curriculum teach?

Content, and the skills to handle it: reading primary sources, asking why something happened, weighing whose story is told, and making an argument with evidence. A course that only covers names and dates leaves out the part colleges and AP exams care about most.

Should my homeschooler take AP U.S. History or AP World History?

Both are history courses with a lot of overlap in skills: reading sources and writing an argument. Many students choose by interest and by what they have already studied. How a course counts toward a transcript is your family's call under your state's rules. Each AP guide here starts with a check that shows which exam sections need the most work.

Is there a secular homeschool history curriculum for high school?

History Is an Argument is not written from a religious point of view. It teaches students to ask how we know what we know, and your family picks the topics to investigate.

Can a teen do high school history on their own?

Older teens often can. The AP guides are built to be run by the student. History Is an Argument is led by a parent for one session a week, though a motivated 16 or 17 year old can run much of it alone.
