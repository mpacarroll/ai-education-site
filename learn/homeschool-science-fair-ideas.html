Homeschool Science Fair Ideas That Are Real Tests

Homeschool science fair ideas that make a real fair test, how to turn a curiosity into a testable question, and a 12-week plan for ages 10 to 14.

2026-11-26

The usual list of homeschool science fair ideas is a list of demonstrations: a volcano, a potato battery, a crystal garden. They look good on a table, and none of them tests anything unless your child changes one thing on purpose and measures what happens. The ideas below are starting points, and each one turns into a real experiment once it has a variable.

This page is for parents of children about 10 to 14 getting ready for a science fair, a co-op fair or a family science night.

Who this is for

  • Parents whose child has a fair coming and no question yet.
  • Families who want a real experiment, not a craft.
  • Parents without a science background who need a plan to follow.

Science fair ideas that test something

Each of these changes one thing (the independent variable) and measures one result (the dependent variable). Keep everything else the same.

  • Plants and light. Do bean seedlings grow taller under different colors of light? Measure height every two days.
  • Paper towels. Which brand holds the most water? Weigh the water each sheet holds, three trials each.
  • Ice melting. Does salt, sugar or sand melt ice fastest? Time each cube on the same plate.
  • Paper airplanes. Does wing size change flight distance? Same paper, same throw, ten flights each.
  • Bread mold. Does bread mold faster in the fridge, on the counter or in a bag? Photograph it daily. Keep the bags sealed and throw them out unopened.
  • Ball bounce. Does a ball bounce higher when it is warm or cold? Drop it from the same height and record each bounce.
  • Reaction time. Is your family's reaction time faster in the morning or the evening? Use the ruler-drop test.

Turning a curiosity into a question

A testable question names what changes and what is measured: "Does the amount of sunlight change how tall a bean plant grows in two weeks?" Your child should be able to say their guess and why before starting. Then plan the trials, run them more than once and write down every number, including the ones that do not fit.

Questions parents ask

What makes a good homeschool science fair project?

A question your child actually wonders about, that can be tested by changing one thing and measuring another. A volcano model shows a reaction, but it does not test anything. Add a variable, such as the amount of vinegar, and it becomes an experiment.

Are there homeschool science fairs near me?

Many homeschool co-ops, libraries and regional fairs accept homeschool entries. Ask your local homeschool group or library first. Our plan works for any fair, or for a family science night at home.

How long does a science fair project take?

Plan for several weeks: time to choose a question, read about it, design the test, run enough trials and make the board. Our guide spreads it over 12 weekly sessions, with the project itself in the last three.

How much should I help with the project?

Supervise safety and ask questions. Let your child choose the question, make the guess and explain the results. A judge will ask them, not you.
