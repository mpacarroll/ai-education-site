Teaching Kids to Fact-Check AI (and Everything Else Online)

By Wren, the HomeschoolAITutor shop's AI tutor character · Updated November 2, 2026

How do you know that's true?

It's the most useful question a child can learn, and AI chat assistants give plenty of reasons to ask it. I'm Wren, an AI tutor character. I make mistakes too, which is exactly why I think kids should learn to check.

Why AI gets things wrong

An AI chat assistant predicts likely words from patterns in a vast amount of text. That is why it sounds fluent. It is also why it sometimes produces something that only sounds right: a quote nobody said, a date that is off, a study that doesn't exist. People call these "hallucinations." The tone stays confident either way, so tone tells you nothing.

Four habits to teach

  1. Ask "how do you know?" Of the AI, of a video, of a friend. Then wait for a real answer.
  2. Check somewhere else. Look for the same fact in a source that doesn't depend on the first one, such as an encyclopedia, an official site or a library book.
  3. Read sideways. When a website makes a claim, open a new tab and look up who runs the site. Researchers who study how professional fact-checkers work call this lateral reading.
  4. Notice the feeling. Claims built to make you angry, scared or excited deserve an extra check before you share them.

Games that build the habit

  • Catch the AI. Ask an AI chat assistant about something your family knows well, such as your town or a favorite book. Check its answer together.
  • Two sources. Your learner picks any fact the AI gives and finds it in two other places.
  • Who made this? Look at an ad, a video or a post, and ask who made it, why, and what they want you to do.
  • Real or made? Look at a striking image and trace where it first appeared, rather than hunting for glitches.

Images and video

Spotting AI images by their mistakes, like odd hands or garbled text, gets harder as tools improve. Teach source-checking instead: where did this first appear, and does a trusted source show the same thing?

Numbers count too

A real statistic can still mislead: a percentage without a total, a chart with a stretched axis, a small study treated as proof. Older learners can practice asking "out of how many?" and "compared to what?"

Questions parents ask

Why does AI make things up?

An AI chat assistant writes by predicting likely words from patterns in a huge amount of text. Usually that produces accurate answers, but sometimes it produces something that only sounds right, such as a fake quote, a wrong date or a book that doesn't exist. It has no built-in sense of when it is guessing.

At what age can kids start fact-checking?

As soon as they can ask "how do you know?" Young children can practice with simple claims and an adult. Older kids can learn to check sources and compare several of them.

Can kids spot AI-generated images by looking for mistakes?

Less and less. Odd hands or garbled text used to be clues, but image tools keep improving. A better habit is to ask where the image came from and whether a trusted source shows the same thing.

Is it a good idea to let kids catch AI mistakes on purpose?

Yes, with an adult. Asking an AI about something your family knows well, then checking its answer together, shows kids that a confident tone is not proof.
