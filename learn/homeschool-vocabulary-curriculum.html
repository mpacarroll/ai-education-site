Homeschool Vocabulary Curriculum: Roots and Real Use

Choosing a homeschool vocabulary curriculum: context clues, word roots and real use instead of lists, for ages 9 to 13 and teens. Free placement check.

2026-11-26

Most homeschool vocabulary curriculum is a weekly list: twenty words, their definitions, a few sentences and a quiz on Friday. It works for the quiz. A month later most of the words are gone, and the child still has no way to handle a word that was never on a list. The part that lasts is the skill of working out a word from its parts and its context.

This page is for parents of children about 9 to 13, and of teens, choosing a vocabulary plan. It covers what to look for and where our two guides fit.

Who this is for

  • Parents whose child skips over hard words while reading.
  • Families tired of word lists that do not stick.
  • Parents of a teen who meets heavy words in science or history reading.

What to look for in a homeschool vocabulary curriculum

Context clues. A child should practice guessing a word from the sentence around it before looking it up.

Roots and families. One root opens a family of words. Prefixes and suffixes let a child predict meaning before checking.

Shades of meaning. Knowing that "glad" and "thrilled" are different is as useful as knowing either word.

Real use. New words need to be said and written, in the child's own sentences, within days.

A word collection. A running list the child keeps, and returns to, makes the habit visible.

Words from their own reading. The words that stick are the ones your child met in a book they chose, or heard in a conversation they cared about. A plan should pull its words from your child's own week, not only from a printed list, so each new word already has a place to live.

Questions parents ask

What is a good way to teach vocabulary at home?

Teach the tools for figuring out a word, not only the word. Context clues, roots, prefixes and suffixes let a child work out words they have never seen. Then give each new word a real use in conversation or writing within the week.

Is there a vocabulary curriculum for high school?

Where Words Come From is written for teens and adults. It teaches Greek and Latin roots, borrowed words and how meanings drift, then spends two weeks on science and legal language. It fits a high schooler who reads a lot of nonfiction.

Are vocabulary lists a waste of time?

Not entirely, but a list memorized for a test tends to fade. Words stick when the child guesses the meaning first, checks it, and then uses the word for real.

How many new words should my child learn a week?

A handful used well beats a long list skimmed. Our younger plan ends with a ten-word challenge: ten words the child picks, uses in real conversation and writing, and presents.
