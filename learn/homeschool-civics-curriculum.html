Homeschool Civics Curriculum, Nonpartisan

A nonpartisan homeschool civics curriculum for ages 12 to 16: how government works, rights, voting and a real project, with a free placement check.

2026-11-26

Most homeschool civics curriculum is a vocabulary list: three branches, checks and balances, how a bill becomes a law, and a test. Teens can pass it and still not see how any of it touches their own street. The part that sticks is seeing government as a system with real effects, and working on one real issue from start to finish.

This page is for parents of teens about 12 to 16 choosing a civics or government plan. It covers what to look for, how to keep it nonpartisan, and where our guide fits.

Who this is for

  • Parents planning a civics or government unit for middle school or early high school.
  • Families who want the subject taught without taking a side.
  • Parents without a civics background who want the explaining done for them.

What to look for in a homeschool civics curriculum

Why before what. Start with why people form governments at all. The branches make more sense once a teen sees the problem they solve.

Rights and responsibilities together. A teen should be able to say what a right protects and what it asks of them.

The real path of a law. Following one bill through, step by step, teaches more than a diagram.

Local and national. Who fixes the pothole and who sets tax rates are different questions. A good course answers both.

Disagreeing well. Civics includes how to argue about a public question with evidence and respect.

A project. A real issue, a real plan and something your teen actually does turns the facts into understanding.

Where our guide fits

How Government Works is a 12-week plan for ages 12 to 16. It is a PDF you run with any AI chat assistant, and the free versions of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot all work. Each week is one scripted session of about 60 minutes that you lead.

The weeks cover why we have government, rights and responsibilities, the three branches, checks and balances, how a bill becomes a law, voting and elections, local versus national government, being an active citizen beyond the ballot box, and disagreeing well in a democracy. The last three weeks are a civics project: your teen picks a real issue, plans it, does it and presents it.

The tutor rules keep the AI nonpartisan and fact-based, and stop it from handing over answers. A rescue prompt covers the moment an AI drifts toward an opinion.

The project weeks are where the course comes together. Your teen might ask why a crosswalk is missing near a park, find out which office decides, write to it, and report what happened. The issue is theirs to choose, and the guide keeps the work on facts and process rather than on which side is right.

What it does not do

  • It does not take a side on any issue, and it does not tell your teen how to vote.
  • It does not cover your state's rules in detail. Your teen researches those.
  • It is not a full year-long government textbook or an AP U.S. Government course.

Questions parents ask

Is there a homeschool civics curriculum for high school?

How Government Works is written for ages 12 to 16, so it covers middle school and the early high school years. An older teen who wants more depth can pair it with a government textbook and use the final project as the applied part of the course.

Is there a free homeschool civics curriculum?

Government websites, libraries and court and legislature visitor pages are free and useful. What they do not give you is a week by week plan. Our civics placement check is free and shows where your teen stands before you choose.

How do I keep civics nonpartisan at home?

Teach how the system works and how to weigh a claim, not which side to take. Our guide's tutor rules keep the AI strictly nonpartisan, and a rescue prompt covers what to do if it ever takes a side.

Does this cover my state government?

One week compares local and national government and who is responsible for what. Your teen researches your own town and state in that week and in the project.
