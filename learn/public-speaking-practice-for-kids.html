Public Speaking Practice for Kids at Home

A home routine for a child who freezes in front of people: a practice audience, one big idea, a beginning, middle and end, and a real talk by week twelve.

2026-11-02

Most children who dread speaking in front of people have never been given a safe place to practice. School offers a presentation and an audience of peers, which is the hardest possible setting to learn in. Home can offer the opposite: a tiny audience, a short talk, and as many attempts as it takes. An AI tutor adds a patient coach who asks about one thing at a time. Here is the routine.

Start with an audience that cannot judge

Week one in our speaking guide is called Meet Your Practice Audience, and it means that literally. A row of stuffed animals, a pet, a parent who has promised to say nothing until asked. The AI tutor is part of that audience in text. The goal of the first hour is only to say something short, out loud, to something, and then report how it felt. That is it. No evaluation.

Name the nerves before fixing the talk

Week two is about the nerves, not the content. The tutor asks what happens in the body before speaking: the hands, the breath, the voice. Then it asks what the learner already does that helps. Children who can name what fear feels like get a handle on it. The point is not to remove nerves. It is to make them a known quantity so they stop being the whole story.

One big idea, then a shape

A child who is asked to "give a talk about dogs" produces a list. A child who is asked "what is the one thing you want them to know about dogs?" produces a point. Week three works on the one big idea, and week four on a beginning, middle and end. The tutor's rules keep it from writing the talk. It can only ask: what do you want them to remember, what comes first, how does it end. The learner writes every word.

Adding the tools

The middle weeks add one tool each: telling a story inside the talk, using the voice and pauses, standing tall and eye contact, simple visuals, and answering questions from the audience. Each week the learner delivers something short to the practice audience, then tells the tutor how it went, and the tutor asks a question about that one week's tool. A child who is working on pauses does not get notes on posture that week. That is deliberate. Feedback that covers everything fixes nothing.

How the AI gives feedback it cannot see

The AI never hears the talk. What it gets is the learner's report, or yours. That turns out to be useful. Describing what you did with your hands, how fast you went, where the pause landed, is itself a form of self-awareness that most speakers never develop. The tutor asks for specifics: "where were your eyes during the opening line?" A child who answers "on the floor" has just noticed something, and noticing is most of the fix.

Record the practice on a phone if the learner is willing. Watching it back and then answering the tutor's one question is a strong loop. Do not force it. Some children need several weeks before they can watch themselves.

The real talk

Weeks ten and eleven plan and rehearse a real talk, two or three minutes, on a topic the learner chose, to an audience they agreed to. Week twelve is family demo day. The audience should be small and warm. The win is doing it, not doing it well, and that should be said out loud before the talk starts.

What you do

You are the audience, the timer and the person who says nothing until asked. Resist the note. Your child has a coach asking one question at a time; a parent adding four more notes undoes that. After the hour, one sentence of what you noticed going well is plenty.

Questions parents ask

My child will not even read aloud at the table. Is this too much?

The guide starts smaller than that. Week one is about meeting a practice audience, which can be a stuffed animal and the AI, and week two is about the nerves themselves. Nobody stands up in front of anyone until the learner has chosen to.

How does an AI help with speaking? It cannot hear.

It plays the audience and the coach in text. Your child says the talk out loud to you, then types or dictates how it went, and the tutor asks about one thing: the opening, the pause, where the eyes were. The feedback loop is the point, and the AI keeps it to one question at a time.

What age is this for?

The speaking guide is for learners about 9 to 13. Younger children do well with the storytelling guide first. Older learners often move on to the debate guide.

What is the final talk?

Whatever your child chooses in week ten. Two to three minutes, to family or a small group, on a topic they picked. Weeks ten and eleven plan and rehearse it. Week twelve is the talk.
