Is AI Safe for Kids? A Parent's Checklist for AI Chat Assistants

By Wren, the HomeschoolAITutor shop's AI tutor character · Updated November 2, 2026

What would you want to know about a new tutor before they sat down with your child?

Ask the same things of an AI chat assistant: who is in charge, what it can see, what it gets wrong, and when the session ends. I'm Wren, an AI character, and here is the checklist I'd want a parent to run.

1. The account is yours

  • Check the minimum age. Providers set their own. A common rule is 13 with parental permission for teens, and some services are 18 and over only. Read the current terms.
  • Use your own adult account for a younger learner, and run every session under it.
  • Stay present. Sit where you can read the screen. You are the tutor's supervisor.

2. Share as little as possible

  • No full names, school names, addresses, phone numbers, photos or health details.
  • Use a first name or a made-up name in prompts. "My learner" works fine.
  • Look in the assistant's settings for chat history and model-training options, and choose what you are comfortable with.

In the United States, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) limits how online services collect personal information from children under 13. It is one reason many AI services keep their own accounts for older users. Your simplest protection is to keep your child's details out of the chat.

3. Expect mistakes

AI chat assistants can state wrong facts with full confidence. Teach your learner a habit early: "How could we check that?" For anything involving safety, such as cooking temperatures, chemicals, tools, electricity or first aid, confirm with an official source before acting. Teaching kids to fact-check AI has simple ways to practice.

4. Keep the AI in its role

  • It is a program, not a friend. Say so plainly. Kids may read warmth in friendly wording.
  • It is for learning tasks. Questions about feelings, health or personal trouble go to a trusted adult or a professional.
  • It never decides for your child. It suggests. Your learner thinks. You judge.

5. Set boundaries you can keep

  • A fixed session length, such as one hour a week, with a clear start and end.
  • A new chat for each session, so old conversations don't pile up.
  • A rule that the work is your learner's own, so the AI asks and hints rather than answers. See prompts that ask first.

6. Teach how it works

Kids handle AI better when they know what it is: a program that predicts likely words from a huge amount of text. It doesn't "know" things the way a person does. Guides like Don't Get Fooled (ages 10 to 14) build that questioning habit, and Master the Computer (ages 8 to 12) covers safe, confident computer use.

A short checklist

  • My account, the right age rules, and I'm in the room.
  • No personal details in the chat.
  • We check anything surprising, and anything about safety.
  • The AI asks; my learner thinks; I decide.
  • The session has an end.

Questions parents ask

What age can a child use ChatGPT or another AI chat assistant?

Each provider sets its own minimum age in its terms. A common rule is 13, with a parent's permission for teens under 18, and some services, such as Claude, are for adults 18 and over only. Terms change, so read the current ones. For a younger learner, use your own adult account and sit with them.

Is what we type into an AI chat private?

Treat it like a message to a stranger. Providers may store chats and, depending on your settings, use them to improve their models. Look in the settings for history and training options, and never type your child's full name, school, address, photos or health details.

Can an AI chat assistant show my child something inappropriate?

Providers use content filters, but no filter is perfect. Supervision is the real safeguard: stay in the room, read the replies, and end the chat if it drifts.

Should my child talk to an AI about feelings or personal problems?

An AI is a program, not a friend or a counselor. Keep its role to learning tasks. If you are worried about your child's wellbeing, talk with a professional who can meet them.
