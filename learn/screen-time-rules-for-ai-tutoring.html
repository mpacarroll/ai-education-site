Screen Time Rules for AI Tutoring at Home

How to run an AI tutor without it becoming more screen time: the bounded hour, the adult account, the no-answers rule, and what happens off the screen.

2026-11-02

A fair worry about an AI tutor is that it is one more screen. It is. The question is whether the hour at that screen is doing something the rest of the day does not. Here are the rules we run by, so that an AI tutor stays a lesson and does not drift into another open tab.

Rule 1: The hour has edges

One session, one hour, one week. It has a start, when the prompt is pasted into a fresh chat, and an end, when the tutor's closing task is set and the tab is closed. The guides are built that way, and it is the most important rule here. A tutor session with no end becomes a chat, and a chat becomes browsing.

Put the hour on the calendar like a music lesson. Hold it. The regularity is what makes it a lesson in the learner's mind rather than a screen they can open when bored.

Rule 2: The account belongs to the adult

Create the account in your name and run every session under it. AI providers set minimum ages for account holders. The practical effect is that the chat is not on your child's device and not available between sessions without you. That single fact does more for screen-time limits than any timer.

Rule 3: The AI never hands over the work

The reason an AI tutor can be more than screen time is the rules block at the top of every weekly prompt. It tells the assistant to ask before it tells, to keep replies short and end them with one question, and never to produce anything the learner could copy to finish the work. Without those rules, an hour with a chat assistant is an hour of reading answers, which is the worst kind of screen time: passive, and it looks like learning.

With the rules, the learner is typing, answering, trying things and reporting back. The screen is a conversation partner, not a feed.

Rule 4: Most of the work happens off the screen

Look at what the guides actually ask for between sessions. A guitar guide wants ten minutes a day with the instrument. A cooking guide wants a meal. A writing guide wants a page written by hand or in a document, not in the chat. A science guide wants an experiment at the kitchen table. The hour with the tutor plans and reviews. The learning happens in the week, away from the chat.

If you find the between-session work drifting back into the AI, that is the signal to tighten up. The chat is closed between sessions.

Rule 5: The adult is present, and the amount changes with age

For younger learners the adult types. For middle-grade learners the adult reads along. For teens, many families move to reading the transcript afterward. There is no right line, but there is a wrong one: a young child alone with an open chat assistant. The AI cannot see who is typing and cannot tell a joke from a worry.

Rule 6: Teach the skill of using it well

The best protection against mindless screen time is a learner who understands what the tool is and how to use it on purpose. Three family guides work on exactly that:

  • How to Study, for learners about 11 to 15, which covers how learning works, notes, remembering instead of cramming, planning a week and beating procrastination. A learner with a study system uses a tutor as a tool inside it rather than as a substitute for it.
  • Big Kid Skills, for learners about 8 to 12, practical independence that mostly happens away from any screen.
  • Master the Computer, for learners about 8 to 12, including accounts, passwords and what to share online, which is the foundation for using any assistant safely later.

Rule 7: Check the fit before you commit

Every guide has a free 15-minute placement check. For the study skills guide, it runs as a conversation about how your learner currently studies and names the week to start at. Running a check is also a bounded, supervised way to see how your learner behaves with an AI before you decide how much of it belongs in your week.

The honest summary

An AI tutor is screen time with a structure: an edge, an adult account, a rules block and a week of off-screen work around it. Keep those four things and the hour earns its place. Lose them and it is a tab like any other.

Questions parents ask

Does an AI tutor count as screen time?

It is time at a screen, so yes. The difference is what happens there. A tutor session is a conversation in which the learner does the thinking and much of the work happens off the screen: on paper, with an instrument, in the kitchen. We still recommend keeping it bounded.

How long should a session be?

The guides are built around one hour a week with the tutor. Shorter is fine, and the rules tell the AI that when time is short it should cover less rather than do more of the work.

Should my child have the chat open while doing homework?

We would not. An open chat during independent work turns into an answer machine. Use the tutor in its hour, close it, and let the between-session work happen without it.

Can an older teen run sessions alone?

The account stays yours and the transcript stays reviewable. Beyond that, how much you sit in is a family call. Many parents move from typing to reading along to checking the transcript afterward as the learner gets older.
